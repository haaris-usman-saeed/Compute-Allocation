Where the GPUs go

A small simulation of how a firm might share a scarce GPU cluster between competing teams, and a cheap fix for one common way of doing it.

The setup

Eight teams share 64 GPUs. Trading teams send small, urgent, valuable jobs. Research teams send large jobs with loose deadlines and uncertain payoff. Backtest teams sit in between. A job is only worth something if it finishes before its deadline, and total demand is about one and a half times what the cluster can run.

Every team gets the same budget of an internal currency. Four schemes compete to turn those budgets into a schedule: priority tiers, where higher tiers cost more and run first; the same tiers with a free backfill queue for leftover capacity; a clearing price auction run every slot; and fair share, where each team is guaranteed an equal slice.

Results are compared against a planner that knows every job's true value and schedules the most valuable work first. That planner is a strong reference, not a true optimum.

This was prompted by public reporting that Jane Street allocates GPU time through an internal bidding currency with priority levels, and that it cannot get all the compute it wants in one place. It is a stylized model with invented parameters, not a description of any real firm's system.

What I found

Budgeted priority tiers leave GPUs idle

When a team runs out of currency, its jobs stop running even while GPUs sit free. Letting leftover capacity go to waiting jobs at no cost recovers about ten points of value, and was worse than plain tiers in only 3% of runs.

Budgets make lying self defeating

A team that inflates its priority, on every job or only on jobs close to their deadline, burns its budget faster and ends up worse off on average. The currency does the incentive work that a rule against gaming would otherwise have to.

Big, flexible jobs lose everywhere

Urgent small jobs win nearly every slot, so research teams get between 43% and 77% of the value the planner would give them, depending on the scheme. None of the four schemes protects long horizon work well.

What this does not show

The parameters are invented, so the numbers show direction rather than size. Jobs here can be split freely across slots, while real GPU jobs often need all their hardware at once. And budgets are fixed for the whole run. If unspent currency expired each period, inflating priority might start to pay, which is the first thing I would test next.
